Too many alerts, too few people

Most of what your monitoring tools raise is harmless. A few alerts are not, and they look the same until someone digs. That digging is slow, it depends on who is on shift, and it is hard to show an auditor afterwards.

The Bank of Ghana's 2026 cybersecurity directive expects regulated institutions to detect threats and respond to them. Good analysts are hard to find, and most smaller institutions cannot staff a team around the clock. We built Hydra to do the first pass on every alert, and we are building the service around it.

What Hydra does with an alert

  1. It arrives. Your monitoring tools send the alert to Hydra: Wazuh, Splunk, Microsoft Defender and others. Hydra keeps the full original event.
  2. It checks the event. Hydra looks at what the event itself shows. Did a program in a user's Downloads folder read the memory that holds passwords? Did Word start PowerShell with an encoded command? 35 patterns like these are built in, and each one is explained in plain words.
  3. It looks around. It finds other alerts on the same machine or user, and what your analysts decided about similar alerts before.
  4. It writes a verdict. An AI model reads what Hydra gathered and writes one of three verdicts: attack, harmless, or needs a person. Every verdict lists the evidence it relies on.
  5. Rules check the AI. Hydra's own rules can overrule the model. If the event shows a strong attack pattern, the model cannot dismiss it. If the evidence is thin, the verdict is "needs a person".
  6. A person decides. Hydra may recommend isolating a computer or disabling an account. Nothing happens until someone with the right role approves it, and every decision is logged.

An investigation, opened

The evidence score runs from 0 to 100 and measures how much evidence points to an attack. It is not a probability. It rises when the event shows attack patterns or other alerts on the same machine or user agree. It falls when the event matches routine activity.

You stay in charge

Hydra recommends. You decide. A person with the right role approves every action before anything runs. A kill switch stops all actions at once. Every decision goes into an audit log in which a changed or deleted entry can be detected.

Who runs it

Today you run Hydra. Your analysts read what it writes and decide. That is how we are testing it.

The plan is for us to run it for you. As we read the Bank of Ghana's 2026 directive, the security monitoring system an institution subscribes to has to be run by a company accredited by the Cyber Security Authority and owned mostly by Ghanaians. So we are not planning to sell you software and step back. Radiant is preparing to apply for that licence. Hydra will do the first look at every alert, and our analysts will handle the ones that need a person.

It would come as one annual subscription, set by your institution's Bank of Ghana tier, covering the software, the operation and the reports the regulator asks for. We have not fixed prices, and nobody pays us yet.

Connectors we have built

Hydra can read alerts from Wazuh, Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, Microsoft Defender, CrowdStrike Falcon, Microsoft Entra ID, Okta, Google Workspace, AWS GuardDuty and Google Cloud, and from an on-premises Active Directory relay. A Palo Alto Networks connector can block an address once a person approves it.

Real events have reached Hydra in our own lab from Wazuh, Okta, CrowdStrike Falcon, Splunk and an Active Directory relay. The other connectors are built, most of them with automated tests, and we will prove each one with the first team that uses it.

On our list, not built yet: SentinelOne, VMware Carbon Black, Cisco Umbrella, Fortinet, Elastic, Sumo Logic, ServiceNow, PagerDuty, Jira, Qualys.
