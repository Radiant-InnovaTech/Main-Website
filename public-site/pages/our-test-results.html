Our model comparisons

We tested Hydra with Qwen3.8-27B, Foundation-Sec 8B and Granite 4.1 3B. Below are the comparisons on matching cases, followed by the earlier 42-alert test. Different case sets and engine versions cannot be ranked as one accuracy score.

Latest Qwen runs measured 25 October 2026; earlier comparison reported 17 October 2026. These are Hydra system results unless marked model-only. Engine v2 is not enabled in production.

Program evidence describes the history of the program involved. Host evidence describes activity on the machine. Why the program-evidence result is an upper bound

The injected history was built from the recordings' harmless events. Attack programs therefore appeared new, while harmless programs with history appeared familiar. This separates the two classes by construction. Real environments also have new harmless programs and attacks using familiar programs. We have not yet measured that fairer mix.

The host-evidence run does not have that particular separation. It confirmed 21 of 26 attacks and cleared 208 of 251 harmless alerts, with none dismissed and no false alarms in the sample. It still uses activity from the period around an attack and repetitive lab background, so it is not a production estimate either.

What we ran

  • Model configuration: Qwen3.8-27B, FP8 (8-bit), thinking switched off, served with vLLM on one NVIDIA A100 40 GB.
  • Investigation: Hydra engine v2, with fixed questions, recorded tool evidence, code-based confidence and guards, and checks on evidence quotes.
  • Cases: recorded Windows attack-log cases from seven lab campaigns, covering one attack family. The harmless alerts are background activity from the lab, not customer traffic.
  • Runs: one run per evidence setting, on the same 277 cases. No model was rerun to prepare this publication.
  • Verification: the published counts were recomputed from the individual saved outcomes. They are final Hydra decisions, not the model's unchecked answers.

What these results do not prove

  • Zero observed errors is not a guarantee. There are only 26 attack cases. No attacks dismissed in this sample does not establish a zero dismissal rate in use.
  • This is a narrow lab test. It does not establish performance across other attack families, operating systems, cloud alerts or customer environments.
  • These are system results. The model, behaviour rules and guards work together. The counts should not be presented as Qwen's standalone accuracy.
  • It is not an independent evaluation. The team prepared the cases and labels. External relabelling and adversarial review are still needed.
  • Each configuration was run once. We have not measured Qwen's variation across repeated runs here.
  • It is not a bank burst test. These counts do not establish production capacity, response-time guarantees or cost per customer.
  • It is not the production configuration. Engine v2 remains disabled in production, and the Qwen GPU stays off unless a separate run is requested.

What comes next

We plan a fairer history baseline, independent review of labels and rules, and a separate burst test. Customer work with real data will not start until the Cyber Security Authority answers our licence position in writing.

Earlier comparison: 42 alerts, October 2026

These previously published results are retained for comparison. They use 18 attacks and 24 harmless alerts, with a different setup from the newer test. Qwen was not measured on this set. Granite was run before the later fixes, so this is not a controlled ranking of all three models.

The test

  • 42 alerts: 18 real attacks and 24 harmless look-alikes.
  • 20 from recorded real-world data. These come from public attack recordings (OTRF Security-Datasets, MIT licence). The attack tool's own events are labelled as attacks. The other events from the same recordings are labelled harmless.
  • 22 we wrote ourselves from well-known attack and normal patterns: a Word document starting PowerShell, Kerberoasting, password spraying, backup software, patching, VPN logons and so on.
  • Titles and severity do not give away the answer. Alerts are titled and rated by the rule that fired, never by the label, and a test checks that severity does not separate attacks from harmless alerts within a rule.
  • A new customer's situation. We removed anything a brand-new customer would not have yet: stored reputation lookups, related alerts and analyst history.

The five attacks it did not flag

We list them because you should know where the checks are weak.

  • A memory-dumping tool running from a user's Desktop folder, seen on its own as a program starting. Hydra only noted that it ran from a folder anyone can write to.
  • A reconnaissance command (queries about the user, the system or accounts). Hydra noted it as weak evidence.
  • A program listening for connections on port 8000.
  • Two reads of the Windows Credential Manager vault.

Each of these scored too low for Hydra to call an attack in that earlier run, so each went to a person. These failure cases belong to the 42-alert test, not the newer 277-case test.

Five harmless alerts were also left to a person rather than cleared: two ordinary logons, two process starts and one registry change.

What this test does not show

  • We wrote 22 of the 42 cases, knowing which checks existed. That flatters the result.
  • Nobody outside the company has relabelled it. The labels are ours.
  • We tuned the checks while looking at half of the alerts. We scored the other half once, with the checks frozen: 3 of 6 attacks flagged and 5 of 9 harmless cleared. We then fixed two bugs, so the current figures on that half (4 of 6 and 6 of 9) are no longer a clean test.
  • It covers Windows events only. It says nothing about Okta, AWS, Google Cloud or Linux alerts.
  • The recordings are small and from 2020. Each has between 68 and 122 events.
  • One computer, two small models. Results with other models or hardware will differ.
  • It does not test the reputation lookups or analyst history, because a new customer has neither.
  • It shows the checks work on these patterns. It does not show how Hydra will do on your alerts.
  • It is small. Zero dismissed among 18 attacks does not prove a zero dismissal rate in use.
